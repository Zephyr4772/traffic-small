A traffic light that thinks

Normal signals run on timers: every road gets the same green, whether it has fifty cars or none. This project simulates a city and a smart signal that decides who goes next from what is actually waiting: what kind of vehicle it is, how long it has waited, what time of day it is, and whether the road ahead has room.

1 · The vehicles and their priority

2 · How the smart light decides

Every 0.1 seconds each junction adds up the points of the vehicles waiting on each road (within 150 m, the range of the detectors):

road score = Σ ( vehicle points × (1 + seconds waited ÷ 30) ) × road-ahead factor

The waiting time term makes points grow the longer a vehicle waits, so a quiet road is never forgotten. The road-ahead factor lowers the score if the road it would drive into is already full, because sending more cars there only blocks the junction.

1
Ambulance first. If an ambulance is waiting, its road gets green after a short safe change (3 s minimum), and keeps it until the ambulance is through.
2
No one waits forever. A green never lasts more than 60 s; after that the busiest other road must get its turn.
3
Switch only when it pays. After at least 7 s of green, the light changes only if another road's score is clearly higher (30% more), because every change wastes about 5 s.
4
Change safely. Every change goes amber 3 s, then all-red 2 s, so the junction is empty before the next road moves.

3 · Timer lights, for comparison

The Fixed-time mode is what most junctions do today: each road gets green in turn for a fixed time (24 s on main roads, 16 s on medium roads, 10 s on small streets), whether anyone is waiting or not. Switch between the two modes in the left panel to see the difference on the same traffic.

  • Timer lights give green to empty roads and make full roads wait.
  • They ignore what kind of vehicle is waiting: an ambulance waits like everyone else.
  • The smart light reacts to real demand, so queues stay shorter.

4 · How the surroundings influence the lights

Time of day
School buses count three times more in school hours. The clock at the top shows the simulated time.
Location
School buses only drive to and from schools; ambulances only between hospitals and homes. So junctions near schools and hospitals see more priority vehicles.
The road ahead
If the exit road is backed up, the light holds that road. Vehicles also never enter a junction they can't leave ("don't block the box").
Neighbouring junctions
Because a full exit road lowers a score, a jam at one junction automatically slows the flow into it from its neighbours instead of spreading.

5 · How vehicles drive

  • Left-hand traffic, as in India. Turning left is the short turn; turning right crosses oncoming traffic.
  • Car-following: each driver keeps a safe gap, speeds up and brakes smoothly (the Intelligent Driver Model, a standard in traffic research).
  • Lanes: drivers move into the correct lane for their next turn.
  • Routes: every trip has a start and a destination building and takes the quickest route (A* search), counting delays at junctions.
  • Unsignalled junctions: the smaller road gives way to the bigger one; at all-way stops whoever arrived first goes first.

6 · What to look at in the simulation

  • The coloured bar across each road at a junction is its light: green, amber or red.
  • Live traffic (right panel): average trip time, delay, how many vehicles are standing still, and ambulance and school-bus trip times. Compare these between the two modes.
  • Click any vehicle to follow it and see where it is going and how long it has waited.
